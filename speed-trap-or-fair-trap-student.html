The situation

A real, live fight over what a speed camera should measure

Automated speed cameras are spreading fast across the U.S. right now. Florida has rolled out school-zone cameras statewide, and Los Angeles is preparing to activate its own network. Drivers are fighting back — some call it a genuine safety measure, others call it "a money grab." Almost nobody is asking the physics question underneath it all: what is the camera actually measuring?

Most cameras clock instantaneous speed — one snapshot, one point on the road. Your city is piloting something different: a point-to-point system that records a car entering and leaving a zone, then calculates average speed from distance and time. You're the investigation team deciding whether that system is actually fair.

Your tool

The pilot corridor

average speed = distance ÷ time

Corridor length: 1.0 mile. Posted limit: 45 mph. Two cameras mark the exact second each car enters and exits. At the legal limit, a car should take 80 seconds to cross — faster than that, and its average speed is over 45 mph.

Four cars, four crossing times

Before you report back

Two of these cars are going to spark real disagreement in the room. If your group is torn on a verdict, that's not a sign you did the math wrong — write down both sides of the argument and be ready to make your case.

One more thing to think about

Distance, or displacement?

Here's a detail that hasn't come up yet: the school zone isn't perfectly straight — it curves slightly along the road. The distance a car actually drives through the curve is 1.0 mile, but the straight-line displacement between the two camera points is only 0.92 miles.

Discuss with your group: if the system used displacement instead of distance for Car 1's calculation, what would happen to its result? Would Car 1's verdict change? Be ready to explain your reasoning — including which quantity (speed or velocity) each version of the calculation would actually represent.
