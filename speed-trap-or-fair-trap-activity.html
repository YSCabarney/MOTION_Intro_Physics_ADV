Speed Trap or Fair Trap?

Your city is piloting a new kind of speed camera. Before it goes live, the council wants to know: does it actually catch dangerous driving — or does it let some of the most dangerous moments slip through completely legal?

Why this activity

A real, unresolved 2026 fight — and your notes already hold the physics behind it

Automated speed cameras are spreading fast across the U.S. right now. Florida has rolled out school-zone cameras statewide, and Los Angeles is preparing to activate its own network. Drivers are fighting back — some calling it a genuine safety measure, others calling it "a money grab." Both sides are arguing about fairness. Almost nobody is asking the physics question underneath it: what is the camera actually measuring?

Nearly every camera in that real debate clocks instantaneous speed — one snapshot, one point on the road. But a different system already exists: point-to-point cameras that record a car entering and leaving a zone, then calculate average speed using the exact formula from your notes — distance ÷ time. Today, your class pilots that second system and has to decide if it's actually fair.

Run of show

Timing for a 30-minute period

  • 0–4 min Frame the case

    Share the real headline live: speed cameras expanding fast in 2026, drivers calling it unfair. Ask: "A camera says a car was speeding. What would you want to know before you trust that ticket?" Take a few answers before introducing the two camera types.
  • 4–7 min Set up the pilot corridor

    Put the corridor details on screen (below): a 1-mile school zone, 45 mph limit, two cameras marking entry and exit time. Work through how average speed gets calculated together as a class.
  • 7–20 min Case work

    Working solo or in pairs (however the group splits best at this size), students calculate average speed for all 4 cars and decide: ticket or no ticket. Each car comes with a short driver's account of what actually happened in the zone.
  • 20–26 min Report back and debate

    Go car by car. Car 3 and Car 4 should spark real disagreement — let the group argue before you weigh in.
  • 26–30 min The twist, and the bridge to next class

    Reveal the distance-vs-displacement twist (below) and connect it to the vector/scalar distinction from the notes — setting up why real systems have to be careful about which one they use.

The pilot corridor

average speed = distance ÷ time

Corridor length: 1.0 mile. Posted limit: 45 mph. Two cameras mark the exact second each car enters and exits. At the legal limit, a car should take 80 seconds to cross — faster than that, and its average speed is over 45 mph.

The point of Cars 3 and 4

They're mirror-image problems with the same tool. Car 3 shows how average speed can hide a genuinely dangerous instant. Car 4 shows how it can penalize a driver who did exactly the safe thing. Neither is a math error — it's a real limitation of what the system can see. Let the group sit with both before resolving it; there isn't a clean "right" verdict for either car, only a clearer sense of what the system trades away.

The twist — save for the last few minutes

Distance, or displacement?

Here's the detail that didn't come up yet: the school zone isn't perfectly straight. It curves slightly along the road. The distance a car actually drives through the curve is 1.0 mile — but the straight-line displacement between the two camera points is only 0.92 miles.

Ask the class: if the system used displacement instead of distance for Car 1's calculation, what would happen?

Reveal

0.92 mi ÷ 75 sec works out to about 44.2 mph — suddenly under the 45 mph limit. The same car, same 75 seconds, goes from a clear ticket to no ticket at all, purely depending on whether the system measured distance (making it speed) or displacement (making it velocity). That's not a rounding error — it's the scalar/vector distinction from your notes deciding a real legal outcome. Real point-to-point systems have to deliberately use distance along the road, not straight-line displacement, specifically so curves and bends can't be used to dodge a ticket.

Debrief prompts

Closing the loop

  • Was Car 3 actually safe? Was Car 4 actually dangerous? Does the math answer that, or just the driver's account?
  • If you sat on the city council, would you approve this camera system? What would you want changed first?
  • Why does it matter, legally, whether a speed system uses distance or displacement?

This sets up instantaneous vs. average velocity, and the scalar/vector distinction, as tools that already have real stakes attached — not just abstract definitions. It's a natural bridge into acceleration next, since Cars 3 and 4 both involved a change in speed mid-trip that the average alone couldn't capture.
