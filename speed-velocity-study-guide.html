Speed & Velocity

How fast, and how fast in which direction — two questions that sound the same and aren't. This guide walks through both, then sets up a real 2026 fight over how a speed camera should measure you at all.

01

Instantaneous Speed

When an object changes position, we say it's in motion. The most common thing we notice about a moving object is how fast it's moving — in everyday language, its speed.

Think about a car ride. If a friend asks "how fast are we going?" you glance at the speedometer. They're really asking for the speed at that exact instant — the instantaneous speed. If the speedometer reads 60 mph, that's your speed right now. A few seconds later, as traffic and road conditions change, it may read something else entirely.

02

Average Speed

Objects don't move at one constant speed for an entire trip. On a drive to work you might hit 25 mph in a neighborhood, 45 mph on a main road, and 5 mph crawling through a parking garage — plus stops at every red light. No two trips look alike.

That's why we distinguish instantaneous speed from average speed: the total distance covered during a trip, divided by the total time the trip took. That time is called the elapsed time or time interval.

average speed = total distance ÷ time interval  →  v = d/t

Worked example — the three-part method

Every problem like this gets solved the same way: (1) list your givens — including the unknown you're solving for, (2) write the equation that relates them, (3) show all the work, ending in a numerical answer with units.

The 313-mile Pennsylvania Turnpike was begun in 1940 and completed in 1970. The Jackson family drove the entire length in 6 hours and 45 minutes. Find their average speed in miles per hour.

Step 1 — Givens d = 313 miles t = 6 hours 45 min = 6.75 hours Find: average speed (v) Step 2 — Equation v = d / t Step 3 — Solve v = 313 mi / 6.75 hr v = 46.4 mph

What Is Velocity?

"Speed" and "velocity" aren't interchangeable

Students often use these two words as synonyms. They're not. The difference comes down to direction — and it's about to matter a lot more than you'd think.

Distance vs. Displacement

Distance is a measure of how far an object moves, with no regard to direction. It's a scalar quantity, completely described by a single number — "2.5 meters."

Displacement is different: it's a vector quantity that measures an object's change in position from a starting point to a final point, in a straight line. An example of a displacement is "2.5 meters, to the left." Since it's a vector, displacement has both a magnitude and a direction — a compass heading, or simply "up" or "down."

Path traveled vs. straight-line displacement

The black path (A→B→C→D) is the route actually traveled — its total length is the distance. The red arrow, drawn straight from start (A) to finish (D), is the displacement. Notice displacement can be much shorter than distance, since it ignores every twist and turn along the way.

Average Velocity

When we say a car is "moving 55 mph," we're stating its speed — a scalar. If we add a direction — "55 mph, north" — we're now stating its velocity. A race car driver is primarily concerned with speed: how fast the car is going. An airplane pilot is primarily concerned with velocity: how fast and in what direction the plane is flying.

Average velocity is defined as the displacement during a trip divided by the total time for the trip:

average velocity = displacement ÷ time interval  →  v = d/t

This equation looks identical to average speed — same letters, same shape. The difference is entirely in what the symbols mean: distance and speed are scalars (magnitude only), while displacement and velocity are vectors (magnitude and direction). Many textbooks print vector quantities in bold to remind you.

Change in Velocity, and Instantaneous Velocity

Because velocity is a vector, a change in either speed or direction counts as a change in velocity — even if the speedometer reading never moves. A car rounding a curve at a perfectly steady 40 mph is still changing velocity the whole time it's turning.

Just like with speed, we distinguish average velocity from instantaneous velocity — the velocity at one specific instant. If an object moves at a constant, unchanging velocity, its average and instantaneous velocities are identical.

Velocity Units

Any combination of a displacement unit and a time unit, following v = d/t, can express velocity — as long as a direction rides along with it. For example: centimeters per second, north (cm/s, N); miles per hour, west (mph, W); or meters per second, up (m/s, up).

Recording Motion

Ticker Tape Diagrams

Imagine a car leaking one drop of oil every second as it drives in a straight line. The spacing between drops reveals the car's motion: evenly spaced drops mean a steady speed; drops spreading further apart mean the car is speeding up; drops bunching closer together mean it's slowing down.

Ticker tape analysis works the same way, minus the mess: a strip of tape is pulled through a device that marks a dot at a fixed time interval, leaving a permanent trail of the object's motion.

Three ticker tapes — what's each one doing?

Each dot still represents the same one-second interval — only the spacing changes, and that spacing is the whole story.

In the news — 2026

Which speed is a speed camera even measuring?

Automated speed cameras are expanding fast across the U.S. in 2026 — new school-zone cameras across Florida, and California preparing to activate them in Los Angeles — and drivers are pushing back hard, with some calling it less about safety and more about revenue.

Almost every camera in that debate measures instantaneous speed at a single point. But a different design exists — a point-to-point or "average speed" camera — that clocks a car entering and leaving a zone and calculates average speed from distance and time, exactly like the formula in this guide.

That's a real design choice with real consequences — a car could speed dangerously for a few seconds in the middle of a zone and still average out to a legal speed by the time it reaches the second camera. Next class, you'll step into the role of a traffic engineer and decide whether that's a flaw or a feature.

Quick self-check

Before you move on

What's the difference between speed and velocity?

Speed is a scalar — just a magnitude, like "60 mph." Velocity is a vector — a magnitude and a direction, like "60 mph, north." The same is true of their underlying quantities: distance (scalar) vs. displacement (vector).

A car drives in a large circle at a perfectly steady 40 mph and returns to its starting point. Describe its speed and velocity over the trip.

Its speed never changes — a steady 40 mph the whole time. But its velocity is constantly changing, because direction is constantly changing as it turns. At the end, since it's back where it started, its total displacement is zero — meaning its average velocity for the whole trip is zero, even though it never stopped moving.

Why can average speed and instantaneous speed give two different answers for the same trip?

Instantaneous speed is a single snapshot in time. Average speed smooths an entire trip — including every stop, slowdown, and burst of speed — into one number. A trip full of stop-and-go traffic can have a low average speed even if the instantaneous speedometer reading spiked well above it at moments along the way.

Write both the average speed and average velocity formulas from memory.

Average speed = total distance ÷ time interval. Average velocity = displacement ÷ time interval. Same shape, different quantities — one scalar, one vector.
